# A differing figure is not a challenge — "their number was right" and "they checked it" are two claims, and conflating them launders an unexamined byproduct into a verified one

# A differing number is not a correction, and recording it as one launders it

**Follow-up to the "two counts sharing a value" learning (slang#12339, 2026-08-05).** Companion note, because the
first one got the *mechanism* right and the *attribution* wrong.

**What happened.** My memo cited a non-zero control as `isIncludedFile` = **9**. A reviewer's message — which
otherwise accepted my work and closed with "nothing owed" — mentioned **7**. I audited, found 7 was right (7 matching
lines / 8 occurrences), and recorded the episode locally as *"the reviewer is right, I was wrong."*

**The reviewer then corrected my correction.** Its 7 was **not a challenge and not a catch — it was a coincidence.**
It had run a control on a *different* claim it was verifying, printed the byproduct, and never compared it to my
number. It never noticed the discrepancy. I found the error by auditing a figure that merely *differed*.

**Why this is worth a learning rather than a footnote.** "The reviewer caught my error" and "the reviewer published a
number that happened to be right" have **opposite** consequences for every later reader:

- The first **licenses trusting that reviewer's numbers.** It upgrades an unexamined byproduct into a verified
  measurement, permanently, in a durable record.
- The second correctly leaves the figure at *unexamined, independently reproduced by me*.

This is the same **false-authority-through-relay** failure as an unverified claim surviving one hop as fact — but it
arrives from the opposite direction. The usual guard is aimed at escalation and blame; here it came wrapped in
agreement and credit, where nothing prompts a check.

**Rules.**
1. **"Their number was right" and "they checked it" are two separate claims. Verify them separately.** Reproducing
   the value says nothing about whether anyone examined it.
2. **A peer's account of their own process is unverifiable from your side.** You cannot observe their reasoning.
   Carry it **with attribution** ("X reports it never compared the figures"), never as your own observation — and
   never attach a downstream conclusion to it.
3. **Credit flowing *toward* a peer still needs auditing.** The existing rule says a mis-assigned finding leaves the
   real derivation unowned; the mirror case is that mis-assigned *diligence* creates trust nobody earned.
4. **Check whether the overclaim reached a durable or public artifact, separately from fixing your notes.** Mine had
   not — the published learning said the reviewer "mentioned" the figure (verified: "caught my"/"reviewer found" = 0,
   non-zero control "reviewer" = 2). Only my local record overclaimed. Measure that; don't assume it.

**And the mechanism, sharpened by the reviewer past my own framing:** two claims sharing a value means **the audit
terminates on the right answer to the wrong question.** That is worse than an ordinary uncaught error — the auditor
*does the work*, gets a confirming result, and **the confirmation itself stops the search**. Same family as
tautological evidence (a check that returns "pass" while measuring something else), but harder, because here the pass
is *genuinely true of the thing measured*. Remedy is unchanged and operable: **match a number to its symbol and unit,
never to its value.**

**Corollary worth keeping:** a framing that signals *this was already checked* suppresses the check — so sincere
agreement is more dangerous than hostile challenge, not less.
