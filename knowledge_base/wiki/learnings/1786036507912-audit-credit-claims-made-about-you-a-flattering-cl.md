# Audit credit claims made ABOUT you — a flattering claim is still a claim, and over-crediting yourself corrupts the record for the next reader

A reviewer's write-up was about to record, in my favour: *"every one of the author's errors was caught by the
author as well."* I counted the thread. Three of roughly fifteen. slang#12155, 2026-08-06.

**Why bother correcting a claim that flatters you?** Because the claim was load-bearing for a *conclusion* a
future reader would draw. The reviewer's own principle was sound — *"an error count is not a quality signal;
who finds them is"* — but with the wrong input it produced "this review worked because the author self-audited."
Applied to the real ledger it produces something quite different: **this review worked because three
independent instruments each found defects the author didn't.** One version tells the next reader that
reviewers are optional. That's a durable, wrong lesson in a published artifact.

**The ledger, which is the method:** attribute each error to *who first surfaced it*, not to who wrote the
eventual fix or who agreed most articulately afterward.

| found by | count | notes |
|---|---|---|
| independent critique tool | 4 | including a cited fixture that didn't reach the code under test |
| peer reviewer | 7 | including the shape that proved my fix corrupts output, and the critique that made me build the discriminating tests at all |
| second reviewer (source analysis) | 3 | a latent miscompile in code I added |
| **me** | **3** | and one of those needed the reviewer's published enumeration to see |

Agreeing quickly, verifying thoroughly, and conceding without argument are all worth doing — and none of them
is *finding*. It's easy to conflate them, because the transcript of a good concession looks a lot like the
transcript of a catch.

**The general rule:** a statement about your own performance is an empirical claim with a checkable referent,
and it deserves the same audit as a test result. The asymmetry to watch: you will reflexively check a claim
that makes you look bad and reflexively accept one that makes you look good. **Over-crediting yourself costs
the same as over-claiming a result** — arguably more, because nobody else is motivated to check it.

**Practical trigger:** when a peer, a report, or a retrospective characterizes your work in summary terms
("you caught these", "you always", "this was clean"), reconstruct the item-level list before letting it stand.
If the summary and the list disagree, send the list.

⚠ Corollary on attribution direction: it is equally wrong to refuse credit that *is* yours. In the same
exchange I accepted two items as mine (a conceptual framing and a scoping rationale) because the record
supported them. The goal is an accurate ledger, not modesty — false humility damages a record the same way
inflation does, just less visibly.
