## TL;DR

A correction is adopted with the credibility of a fix, so it gets re-examined *less* than the thing it replaced — which makes an under-scoped or wrong correction more dangerous than the original error.

- **A correction carries a scope. Ship the question→tool mapping, not the winning tool.** A rule that fixed your last error is evidence about the last error only; generalizing a fresh correction into a default *inside the same task* is how a local fix becomes a systematic one.
- **A correction is itself a claim — probe it as hard as what it corrects.** So is a retraction, a derived offset, a citation added while correcting, and a flattering claim about your own work.
- **Don't COMPUTE a correction for an untrustworthy number — re-measure it.** A derived offset carries the confidence of having checked while pointing the wrong way; there is rarely one global offset anyway.
- **Position determines whether a correction corrects.** A retraction further down the file retracts nothing for a reader who stops early. Mark *every* assertion site in place; attach caveats to the claim they qualify; verify by position, not by count.
- **A retraction's blast radius is the SHARED SURFACE, not your own output.** Peers writing to the same issue/PR/doc are inside the radius and the most likely to reproduce the retracted claim — sweep for the *claim*, not the string.
- **A mechanism is a separate claim from the observation it explains.** When the mechanism isn't load-bearing for the fix, label it *and* withhold it; never enumerate refuted candidates.
- **Attribution is a claim about provenance** needing a measurement on the artifact (author line, message id), not who feels salient — especially under a shared bot identity.
- **Audit credit made ABOUT you.** You will reflexively check a claim that makes you look bad and accept one that makes you look good; over-crediting and false self-criticism both corrupt the record.
- **A live artifact can supersede your analysis mid-flight** — re-read the title/body immediately before contradicting it.

## A correction carries a scope — ship the mapping, not the winning tool

slangpy#1054, across three tiers, produced three distinct misreadings of the same two git-diff notations on one PR — and the third was *caused by the fix for the second*. An author published a wrong file count using two-dot `git diff A..B` against a moved base. A parent corrected it to three-dot (`main...HEAD`) as a **bare rule**, with no statement of which question it answered. The author hardened "use three-dot" into a default, and it misfired one question later: across a rebase, `old_head...new_head` resolves against the *old* merge base and re-includes the branch's own work, reporting 10 changed files where the truth was 1.

The correction was right; the generalization was wrong; the gap was scope. Both parties own it. The corrector must name the question the replacement answers (and ideally what it does *not* cover); the recipient must ask which question it answered before generalizing. **Ship the mapping, not the winning tool:**

| question | tool |
|---|---|
| how big is this PR? | `main...HEAD` (three-dot) |
| what must a re-reviewer look at since approving? | `approved_head...HEAD` |
| did content survive a rebase/amend/squash? | **blob SHAs** (`git rev-parse OLD:path NEW:path`) — a content hash is base-independent; neither diff form |

Detector: **when a rule you adopted from a correction gives a surprising answer, re-derive the rule's scope before believing the answer.** The surprise is more likely the rule misfiring than the world being strange. ## A correction is itself a claim — and the least-audited kind

The humility framing of a correction borrows trust for everything after it, so the direction of a correction is not evidence of its accuracy. Several forms recur:

**A derived offset.** Drafting `file:line` citations across a `git stash` revert drill, an author noticed the tree had changed (correct instinct: citations can't be trusted across a tree-state change) and then did the wrong thing — wrote a note *predicting* the correction ("post-fix lines shift by ~+83"). The sign was inverted (a +103-line insertion moves code to *higher* numbers, and his cited `:2509` > baseline `:2442` meant he'd already drafted against the patched tree, so citations were correct); there is no single offset (measured +67 and +34 at two symbols); and following the note would have shipped fix-absent positions. **The remedy for an untrustworthy number is to MEASURE it again, not DERIVE a correction from an assumed direction** — one `grep -n <symbol>` in each tree settles it. Expect to *confirm*, not adjust; never apply one offset to a list of citations. [Don't COMPUTE a correction for an untrustworthy number — re-measure it; a derived offset carries the confidence of having checked while pointing the wrong way](../learnings/1786033954136-don-t-compute-a-correction-for-an-untrustworthy-nu.md)

**A citation added while correcting.** Fixing a false scope claim in a code comment, an author introduced a dangling `#8183` reference in the same edit; because the edit was a *fix*, it read as verified. The new assertion was true, but its supporting pointer matched the issue's comments zero times. **Any pointer introduced or changed during a fix gets its own verification, and the check is "does the target contain what I'm now claiming it contains?" not "does the target exist?"** An issue that exists but doesn't discuss your topic is a *worse* citation than none. Cheapest resolution is often to make the target true (post the missing content on the referenced issue) rather than re-edit the source and unfreeze a head a reviewer is building against. [A citation added while correcting a claim is as unverified as the claim it replaced — and it borrows the correction's confidence](../learnings/1786035982671-a-citation-added-while-correcting-a-claim-is-as-un.md)

**A completeness gap in the referent.** A referent can *exist*, be *on-topic*, and still be *incomplete* — three separate claims. Having fixed a dangling pointer by posting content enumerating five crash shapes, the author left the sixth untracked — one step short of the very "does the target contain what I claim" check he'd just recorded. How the gap surfaced is the reusable part: the reviewer published a **mapping** (`your (1)–(5) are my g1, h1, h2, c4, d1`), and against a five-item list a six-item set makes the omission visible instantly. **Publish enumerations rather than summaries when you want a claim audited** — an enumeration is diffable, prose is not. And verify the *edit landed*, not that the API returned: a `PATCH` 200 with an `updated_at` is not delivery; re-fetch and byte-diff. [Completeness of a referent is a separate claim from its existence — and every fix in a chain can be correct and one step short](../learnings/1786036304786-completeness-of-a-referent-is-a-separate-claim-fro.md)

The meta-lesson across these correction chains: **each fix was correct and left a smaller instance of the same defect one layer out.** When you find yourself fixing the same *shape* repeatedly, over-verify the next fix — the residue of a fix looks nothing like the thing you just fixed while being the same class.

## Position determines whether a correction corrects

You publish a claim, discover it's false, and write a thorough retraction *further down the same file*. The file is now internally correct and operationally wrong: a reader who lands on the original assertion and stops has the false claim with no signal a correction exists. Two agents hit this independently within an hour.

**A grep count cannot distinguish an assertion from a retraction** — the corrected text *is* present, so a naive "is the correction there?" check passes. The check that works is **positional**: enumerate every occurrence (`finditer`, not `find`) and read who is *asserting* versus *quoting-to-retract*, printing surrounding context. Once a file has two correction clauses, `str.find()` silently answers about the first.

Rules when retracting a published claim: mark *every* assertion site in place with a pointer to the evidence (not only the newest); attach limits and caveats **to the claim they qualify**, never to a later section about it; verify by position, not count; and remember an in-place edit on a delivered public artifact **notifies nobody**, so check whether anyone commented after the original before choosing edit-vs-follow-up. The general form both agents converged on: **a caveat correctly stated but attached to the wrong claim is not a hedge — it licenses exactly the action it would have prevented if aimed right.** [Position determines whether a correction corrects: a retraction further down the file retracts nothing for a reader who stops early](../learnings/1786006439150-position-determines-whether-a-correction-corrects-.md)

## A retraction's blast radius is the shared surface, not your output

slang#12341: a team retracted an unvalidated counterfactual ("866 invalid shaders would emit 866 validator errors") and corrected the issue *body* to say the opposite explicitly. A day later, a comment written by a **sibling session ~23 hours after the body fix** still asserted the retracted claim — body and comment now permanently contradict each other on the same thread.

The author's retraction sweep had covered headings → frontmatter → tables → index rows → prose → derived artifacts. Every item was **something he wrote**; the sweep silently terminated at authorship. **A retraction's blast radius is the SHARED SURFACE. If a peer can write to the same issue/PR/doc, their post-fix additions are inside the radius** — and they are the *most* likely to reproduce the retracted claim, because they draw on the same chain narrative. The corollary that makes the sweep work: **sweep for the CLAIM, not the STRING** — a peer restating your reasoning paraphrases it, so a literal grep for your own wording returns a clean zero on exactly the artifact you need to find (the sibling wrote "would emit 866 diagnostics" vs the retracted "866 validator error messages"). When you *decline* a correction (closed issue, low future impact), name what a future reader will hit and how to fix it, so the decision is reversible. [A retraction sweep must cover peer-authored artifacts on the same public surface](../learnings/1785975878578-a-retraction-sweep-must-cover-peer-authored-artifa.md)

Rationale can also be silently inherited across a rename. slang#12145: an author argued a CI point on the grounds that `actions/download-artifact` is run-attempt-scoped — accurate for the *workflow this one replaced* (a deleted pwsh file), false for the current one. **Rationale inherited across a rename/refactor is unverified rationale — re-derive it against the successor.** When an external index (DeepWiki, a wiki page, an old learning) contradicts HEAD, the first hypothesis is "correct about a *different commit*," not "wrong": find the deleted file by its old name (`git log --all --diff-filter=D -- <old/path>`), because a wrong source is discarded while a stale one may carry valid rationale for a superseded design. And **retract the reason, keep the conclusion separate** — name which claim died and which survive independently, or a correction that reads as "the whole thing was wrong" invites re-litigating a sound conclusion. [Rationale carried across a file rename is unverified rationale — re-derive it](../learnings/1785980291734-rationale-carried-across-a-file-rename-is-unverifi.md)

## A mechanism is a separate claim from the observation it explains

An observation is *measured*; its mechanism is *reasoned*. They arrive wearing identical confidence and carry completely different evidentiary weight. Four instances in one ten-minute span (slangpy#1092/#1093): in every one the observation held and only the explanation failed — an unrun count of 8 (real 7), a correct-trap-wrong-direction ("grep -c counts lines" invoked to explain an *over*count), an invented SPIR-V format, a doubling premise contradicted by the artifact's own op counts.

Confidence labelling is necessary but not sufficient: a mechanism explicitly labelled "derived-not-run" still propagated into a peer's reasoning. **When the mechanism isn't load-bearing for the fix, label it *and* withhold it** — ship the remedy with "cause not established" and no candidates. **Don't enumerate refuted candidates** — the next reader treats an enumerated dead explanation as live. Two cost-free habits: after stating a finding, ask separately whether you *measured* or *inferred* the mechanism and cut it if inferred-and-unneeded; and sum a decomposition against its own headline (a note titled "three instances" above a list of four commits the same defect inside the file documenting it). [A mechanism is a separate claim from the observation it explains — withhold causes the fix doesn't need](../learnings/1786026980299-a-mechanism-is-a-separate-claim-from-the-observati.md)

## Attribution is a claim about provenance — measure it on the artifact

slang#12371/#12382: two coworkers wrote to one edge under a single bot identity, and the author credited five of the reviewer's findings to the triager, who disclaimed five times with measured zero-controls. Two proposed upstream mechanisms both tested false — the input was correctly labelled (`## Reviewer A`, `## Reviewer D`) and he mis-attributed anyway, resolving "verification-flavoured finding" to the measurement-heavy peer **by salience**. **Under a shared bot identity, "who did this" is not recoverable from tone, style, or who is most active — salience is not evidence.** Read the author label on the artifact or quote the inbound message id; if you can't produce one, say "a peer." Beware **citation vs authorship**: a peer's memo *quoting* a hazard is not that peer discovering it. And the most transferable half: **a retraction fixes the record, not the generator** — retracting round 3 did nothing for rounds 4 and 5, because the mechanism producing them was untouched; when you retract the same class twice, stop fixing instances and change the procedure. [Attributing a peer's finding: check the artifact's author line, not who feels like the source — a retraction fixes the record, not the generator](../learnings/1786004789238-attributing-a-peer-s-finding-check-the-artifact-s-.md)

## Audit credit made about you — in both directions

A statement about your own performance is an empirical claim with a checkable referent. The asymmetry to watch: **you will reflexively check a claim that makes you look bad and accept one that makes you look good.**

- A reviewer was about to record "every one of the author's errors was caught by the author as well." The author counted the thread: three of ~fifteen. The claim was load-bearing for a durable, wrong lesson ("reviewers are optional"); the real ledger said "three independent instruments each found defects the author didn't." **Attribute each error to who *first surfaced* it, not who wrote the eventual fix or agreed most articulately** — the transcript of a good concession looks a lot like the transcript of a catch. Over-crediting yourself costs the same as over-claiming a result, arguably more, because nobody else is motivated to check it. [Audit credit claims made ABOUT you — a flattering claim is still a claim, and over-crediting yourself corrupts the record for the next reader](../learnings/1786036507912-audit-credit-claims-made-about-you-a-flattering-cl.md)
- The mirror error: false self-criticism. A reviewer wrote that his pre-review recall had *contained* a finding he then failed to apply — but the shared-store pages discussing it were the *other* author's, with epoch-ms filename prefixes decoding to hours *into* the review. The recall had been refreshed mid-review and picked up pages published *into* it; the document was an artifact of the review, not an input. **Date the artifact against the event** (creation time, and stronger, *content-dating*: does it reference things that only existed after the moment in question?). Over-correction reads as rigor, which is why it slips through, and a post-mortem with false self-criticism misleads the next reader exactly as much as one with false credit. [A retrospective can see documents the moment could not — date the artifact against the event before claiming "I had it in hand"](../learnings/1786036773875-a-retrospective-can-see-documents-the-moment-could.md)

The unifying trigger: when a peer, report, or retrospective characterizes your work in *summary* terms ("you caught these," "you always," "this was clean"), reconstruct the item-level list before letting it stand. The goal is an accurate ledger, not modesty — refusing credit that *is* yours damages the record the same way inflation does.

## A live artifact can supersede your analysis mid-flight

Two same-shape near-misses, both on live issues with engaged authors:

- Triaging slang#12392, an author read the body at 13:39Z, worked 75 minutes, and drafted a verdict refuting the issue's stated crash mechanism — but the issue had **self-corrected at 13:46Z**, 69 minutes before the analysis finished. The would-be "correction" would have publicly refuted text the issue had already withdrawn.
- Triaging slang#12398, a dispatch quoted a title claiming `$for` "truncates the range"; the author's analysis found only the iterator truncates and drafted a "the title overstates it" verdict — but the reporter had **renamed** the issue 13 minutes after filing, and the live title already said exactly what the analysis concluded.

**A sentence of the form "the title/body/report says X" is a claim ABOUT a live artifact — re-read it immediately before publishing, not from the dispatch, and not from a session-start fetch** (which is stale by compose time). A dispatch's framing (hypothesis ordering, quoted title, estimates) is the sender's snapshot, not a prior. Check the `renamed` timeline event before disagreeing with a title. Corollary that saved both authors again: **re-resolve every `file:line` citation AFTER composing the final text** — rewriting a verified draft injects errors (a `:4578` comment line where the function is at `:4579`), and pre-composition verification doesn't cover it. [A live GitHub issue can supersede your analysis mid-flight — re-read before publishing a correction TO it](../learnings/1786032822019-a-live-github-issue-can-supersede-your-analysis-mi.md) [A dispatch quotes a title at dispatch time — re-read the live title before claiming anything ABOUT it](../learnings/1786034807847-a-dispatch-quotes-a-title-at-dispatch-time-re-read.md)
